Yes. Leave one hummingbird feeder up through the winter in Georgia, Florida, South Carolina, and Alabama. A stocked feeder will not keep a Ruby-throated Hummingbird from migrating. It can feed a late bird that still needs fat for the next leg. In some yards it also feeds a western hummingbird that is already spending the cold months here. Use the usual nectar, change it before it spoils, and hang it again before dawn if a freeze sent it indoors overnight.

## Will a feeder keep hummingbirds from migrating?

Hummingbirds leave because the days get shorter. [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/hummingbird-feeding-faqs) says they are genetically programmed to head south. They time the trip from day length and the angle of the sun. The [Cornell Lab](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/should-i-stop-feeding-birds-in-fall-so-they-can-start-their-migration/) calls day length the strongest migration cue. Feeders left up do not decide whether a bird starts south. Cornell says to keep a feeder up for several weeks after the last bird, in case a straggler needs one more meal.

[Operation RubyThroat](https://www.rubythroat.org/ResearchHummerVagrantMain.html), at Hilton Pond in York, South Carolina, ties fall departure to day length. Bill Hilton Jr. writes that at most places in the eastern United States, 99.9% of Ruby-throated Hummingbirds are gone by October 15. Adult males do not return north of the Gulf Coast until mid-March. Winter records come especially from south Florida and coastal Texas. [South Carolina DNR](https://www.dnr.sc.gov/news/yr2014/may29/may29_hummingbirds.html) says feeders left up well into fall will not delay migration. Georgia DNR biologists, quoted in the [Statesboro Herald](https://www.statesboroherald.com/local/keep-those-feeders-up/), say the same about a feeder kept through winter. [UGA Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/paulding/2021/06/creating-a-hummingbird-habitat/) says a stocked feeder past October does not hold birds back. The [Banding Coalition](https://www.bandingcoalition.org/projecthummer) puts it simply: the feeder draws in a bird that is already nearby.

A Ruby-throated Hummingbird still in the East in late October is usually a young bird from farther north, the [Cornell Lab](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/do-hummingbirds-migrate-in-flocks/) says. It migrates alone and needs a heavy fat load. When neighbors take feeders in and frost kills the flowers, that bird may use yours for a week or two while it refuels. Cornell also notes that many late birds at eastern feeders are Rufous Hummingbirds from the West. They head southeast instead of straight to Mexico.

The [migration timing guide](/migration) has the regional spring and fall dates. Winter feeding starts after that fall departure window goes quiet.

## Which hummingbirds show up in winter in the Southeast?

From March through October, a hummingbird in the Southeast is almost always a Ruby-throated Hummingbird, the only one that breeds in eastern North America ([Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/ruby-throated-hummingbird)). It is the only species known to nest in Georgia, and it returns between late March and early April ([UGA Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/paulding/2026/02/hummingbirds-will-be-back-soon-are-you-prepared/)). It is Alabama's only breeding hummingbird ([Alabama DCNR](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/ALDNR/bulletins/271e63c)) and, in Florida, by far the most common ([UF/IFAS](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/UW059)).

Most of them leave. UF/IFAS says some may stay in south Florida year-round. Most winter in Mexico and South America, and males are back in Florida in March. By mid-October, most Tallahassee Ruby-throats have crossed the Gulf ([UF/IFAS Leon County](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/leonco/2013/12/26/winter-hummingbirds/)). [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/hummingbird-feeding-faqs) still lists the Gulf Coast and south Florida as regular wintering areas for several species. Away from the immediate coast, the [Carolina Bird Club](https://www.carolinabirdclub.org/hummingbirds/winterhummingbirds.html) finds that very few winter birds are Ruby-throated.

The bird you are most likely to host is a Rufous Hummingbird. Audubon's [field guide](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/rufous-hummingbird) says it wanders east more often than other western hummingbirds. Many show up east of the Mississippi every fall and winter, and many winter along the Gulf Coast. It nests farther north than any other hummingbird, to south-central Alaska. Georgia DNR's Todd Schneider calls it the most common western hummingbird in the state. Those birds breed in the Pacific Northwest and Alaska, and a few swing toward the Southeast instead of Mexico. In Tallahassee, Rufous is the most common winter hummingbird.

On [eBird](https://ebird.org/species/rufhum), an adult male looks almost entirely orange, with a white chest. The throat shifts from red to orange to yellow to lime with the light. Females and immatures show orange on the sides and tail. That orange is the mark the [species guide](/species) compares with Black-chinned and Calliope. Photograph the bird before you name it.

A few other western species turn up too. Audubon says some [Black-chinned Hummingbirds](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/black-chinned-hummingbird) stray east and may winter near the Gulf. Females are almost identical to female Ruby-throats. The [Calliope](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/calliope-hummingbird) is North America's smallest bird, about 3 inches long and a tenth of an ounce. Georgia's first winter record was 1998–99. Alabama has eleven other species on its list, all western breeders. In January 2019, eBird showed Black-chinned, Rufous, and Allen's there. Georgia's list has twelve. Tallahassee banders have handled Calliope, Buff-bellied, Ruby-throated, Black-chinned, Costa's, Broad-billed, and Allen's.

[Southeastern Avian Research](https://southeasternavianresearch.org/hummingbirds/) is direct: these birds are not lost. Many stay in one yard all winter. Some return to the same house later. They can handle freezing nights. If a yard stops being worth the visit, they move on. The Banding Coalition mostly sees Rufous and Black-chinned, from December through February.

## What do I do when the nectar freezes?

A 4-to-1 mix of water to white sugar freezes at 27°F, according to [Southeastern Avian Research](https://southeasternavianresearch.org/hummingbirds/). Above that it may turn slushy. It takes a run of days below 27°F to freeze solid. The [Carolina Bird Club](https://www.carolinabirdclub.org/hummingbirds/winterhummingbirds.html) uses the same line. On those nights, bring the feeder in at dark, when hummingbirds are not feeding, and hang it again before first light. [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/hummingbird-feeding-faqs) says a light freeze may leave the sugar water liquid. Cold nectar does not appear to stun a bird. The real problem is a feeder that is frozen solid at breakfast.

They get through the night in torpor, a deep rest. Heart rate and body temperature drop, which UF/IFAS describes, and Alabama DCNR says body temperature can fall by half. Food has to be waiting at daylight. Operation RubyThroat says to hang the feeder before dawn and, in a hard snap, to rotate two feeders. Keep 4 parts water to 1 part sugar. UF/IFAS warns that a sweeter mix can kill a bird, as can honey and artificial sweeteners. The [nectar recipe](/nectar) is the standard ratio. With no bird yet, Southeastern Avian Research says to fill it barely, and Operation RubyThroat says halfway. A falling level is your clue.

## How often should I change the nectar in winter?

Cool weather slows spoilage. People who run winter feeders still change the nectar on a schedule:

- [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/hummingbird-feeding-faqs): twice a week in cool weather, every three days in temperate weather, and every day or two in heat. Wash with hot water and hydrogen peroxide or a weak vinegar solution. Skip dish soap.
- Southeastern Avian Research: every 3 to 4 days.
- Operation RubyThroat: weekly, on a half-full feeder.
- Carolina Bird Club: about every two weeks in cool weather, because the nectar ferments more slowly. Still 4-to-1.

If a bird is visiting, change it every 3 to 4 days. If the feeder is only waiting, weekly is enough, and two weeks is the longest while the nectar stays clear. Cloudiness, black spots, or a sour smell means it is finished.

Warm weather needs a faster change. UF/IFAS says Florida sugar water can grow bacteria fast enough to kill a bird, so change it every 3 to 5 days. Wash with hot water and white vinegar, not soap or bleach. [UGA Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/paulding/2026/02/hummingbirds-will-be-back-soon-are-you-prepared/) says every 2 to 3 days in warm weather, and still says to keep one feeder up all year.

The recipe stays 1 part white sugar to 4 parts water, with no red dye. Audubon, UF/IFAS, UGA, [Alabama Cooperative Extension](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry-wildlife/hummingbirds-in-alabama/), and South Carolina DNR all use it. They warn that honey can grow a fungus that harms hummingbirds. Nectar is only part of the diet. Cornell's [life history](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/Ruby-throated_Hummingbird/lifehistory) lists insects and sometimes sap. [UF/IFAS Leon County](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/leonco/2013/12/26/winter-hummingbirds/) notes winter birds feeding at Yellow-bellied Sapsucker wells.

## Should I report a hummingbird I see in winter?

Yes. Southeastern Avian Research wants a report of any hummingbird from November 1 through March 15, and it keeps a bander list for the states it covers. Banding happens only if you agree. The Banding Coalition's [Project HUMMER](https://www.bandingcoalition.org/projecthummer) uses November 15 through March 1 for Alabama, Georgia, and Florida, including Atlanta, Tallahassee, and southern Georgia. It bands birds with Hummingbird Research, Inc. In the Carolinas, Operation RubyThroat takes reports from mid-October through mid-March. The Carolina Bird Club asks people to contact researcher Susan Campbell from November 1 to March 15. Most winter birds are plain females or juveniles. Telling the species often comes down to a few feathers.

Georgia DNR wants the same reports. Schneider told the Statesboro Herald that backyard sightings are how the state learned these species were wintering there. Enter the bird in [eBird](https://ebird.org/species/rufhum) too. Alabama DCNR's January 2019 note of Black-chinned, Rufous, and Allen's came from those checklists. A sunny photo, a date, and a county still help if no bander can visit. Many winters, nothing comes. Alabama DCNR says a visit is not guaranteed. The quiet feeder is how the unusual bird gets found.
