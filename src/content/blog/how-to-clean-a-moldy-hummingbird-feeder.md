Discard the nectar today. Take the feeder apart, soak the pieces, and scrub every surface a brush can reach, including the feeding ports and the guards over them. Rinse thoroughly, let the parts dry, and hang it again with fresh nectar. Do that the moment the sugar water turns cloudy or you see black spots. If a bottle brush cannot reach the inside, replace the feeder. [Project FeederWatch](https://feederwatch.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/BirdNotes02-Attracting-Hummingbirds.pdf), from the Cornell Lab, says a feeder you cannot fully reach will soon foster bacteria, fungi, and other harmful organisms.

## Is black mold in a hummingbird feeder harmful?

Yes. Project FeederWatch calls that film black mold. Discard the solution and clean the feeder immediately if the water is cloudy or the mold is visible. Bacteria and fungi grow faster as the temperature rises.

[Clemson HGIC](https://hgic.clemson.edu/17755-2/) horticulturist Barbara H. Smith names the injury. Cloudy or moldy sugar water can cause a fungal infection on a hummingbird’s tongue. The tongue swells, and the bird can starve because it can no longer feed. A [U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2025-01/01.29.2025-learn-more-about-bird-feeding-vyfwc.pdf) handout says mold and fermentation in sugar water can be deadly. [Georgia DNR](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/GADNR/bulletins/831a0a) says summer heat leaves the solution riddled with bacteria and mold that can be transferred to the birds. The [National Wildlife Federation](https://www.nwf.org/Native-Plant-Habitats/Resources/Tips-Tools-and-Videos/Hummingbird-Feeders) says mold and bacteria in a feeder can harm the birds.

The spots are a late sign. In a later [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/how-feed-birds-safely-winter) article, Indiana DNR ornithologist Allisyn Gillet says white or colorful streaks mean the feeder is already past due. Audubon calls sugar water a petri dish for bacteria. Sharon Audubon Center rehabilitator Sunny Kellner says you cannot always see fungus or bacteria. The schedules below are there so the wash happens before a spot shows.

## Should I clean it with vinegar, bleach, or hot water?

Scrub the sugar off, then use a mix a source actually prints. The mixes are not the same. Audubon’s [feeding FAQ](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/hummingbird-feeding-faqs) does not mention bleach. [UF/IFAS in Nassau County](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/nassauco/2017/07/17/q-hummingbirds-come-florida/) says to leave chlorine bleach out. [UGA Extension](https://site.extension.uga.edu/paulding/2026/02/hummingbirds-will-be-back-soon-are-you-prepared/) lists bleach and vinegar at one strength. A Fish & Wildlife quiz and a Fish & Wildlife handout disagree with each other. Each row below is one page, not an average of them.

Clemson’s March 11, 2021 note is the mix written in parts: 1 part vinegar and 2 parts water, then a thorough rinse. Smith says a soap film tastes bad to hummingbirds and could be harmful. Clean the port guards too. A later Clemson sheet, [published February 20, 2025](https://hgic.clemson.edu/washing-bird-feeders/), calls a weekly wash, when no mold is present, “a 4-to-1 vinegar and water solution,” plus a scrub brush. It does not say which number is the vinegar. That 4-to-1 line is for a week with no mold. The 2021 note is the page that writes the vinegar as 1 part to 2 parts water. The 2025 sheet does say to rinse off every bit of bleach residue. Its 1-to-9 bleach soak is in the seed-feeder steps, tied there to salmonella.

[UF/IFAS in Sumter County](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/sumterco/2023/03/06/hummingbirds-flying-in/) also says not to use bleach. [Alabama Cooperative Extension](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry-wildlife/hummingbirds-in-alabama/) says vinegar and hot water, and no detergents. The [Fish & Wildlife quiz](https://www.fws.gov/story/quiz-how-can-you-help-hummingbird) answers with plain water or a weak vinegar solution. It says cleaning products can leave harmful residue, and that spoiled solution can be toxic. The youth handout is the page that allows diluted bleach on a hummingbird feeder, without printing the seed-feeder ratio of 1 part bleach to 9 parts water in those steps. UGA is the page that prints the hummingbird bleach mix as 1 to 10.

Audubon prints no amount of hydrogen peroxide. The vinegar strength it does print is the winter article’s half-and-half soak. Dry the feeder before you fill it. Moisture left inside lets fungus or bacteria flourish.

Refill with new nectar, 1 part white sugar to 4 parts water. Audubon says to discard whatever was still in the feeder. The [nectar guide](/nectar) has the ratio and the ingredients that do not belong in it. Fish & Wildlife says honey mixed with water can cause bacterial and fungal growth.

Above 90°F, Clemson says change the nectar that day, and FeederWatch says it may already be spoiled in two days. Georgia DNR’s summer line is still two to three times a week. Dump it the day it looks cloudy or moldy, whichever row you were following. The National Wildlife Federation uses Audubon’s hot-weather pace and says to clean at every refill. Clemson’s February 20, 2025 sheet says new sugar water two times a week, more often in warmer weather.

[Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/hummingbird-feeding-faqs) also says to clean immediately for mold inside, a sick bird at the feeder, or insects in the nectar. [N.C. Cooperative Extension in Alexander County](https://alexander.ces.ncsu.edu/news/hummingbirds-are-back-tips-on-maintaining-your-feeders/) says to wash every time the water is changed once the heat is regular.

Shade slows it. Smith hangs feeders out of full sun to limit leaks and slow mold. FeederWatch says to use the coolest part of the yard.

Cool weather lengthens the gap. Audubon’s cooler-weather line is twice a week. The [winter feeder post](/blog/leave-hummingbird-feeder-up-winter-southeast) covers a freeze and a feeder that is only waiting. A warm December afternoon still belongs on the short rows. FeederWatch ties the growth to temperature.

## What brush cleans the feeding ports?

The bottle is the easy wash. The ports are a narrow tunnel, and the tongue uses that tunnel.

Clemson’s 2021 note names a bottle brush and the guards on the nectar ports. Alexander County says to take the feeder apart, soak the pieces in hot water, and scrub every part, especially the port holes. A pipe cleaner fits those holes. Then rinse, air dry, and refill. FeederWatch and the Fish & Wildlife handout both name a bottle brush. FeederWatch wants that brush clean, and it wants the bristles on every inside surface. NWF’s routine clean is hot water, a weak vinegar solution, and a scrub brush.

If the brush stops short of the bottom, or it will not enter a port, that surface stays dirty. That is the FeederWatch reason to retire the feeder.

## Which feeders are easier to keep from growing mold?

Choose one you can open. FeederWatch says ease of cleaning is the most important feature, because an unreachable feeder grows bacteria, fungi, and other harmful organisms. NWF says to pick one you can take apart, so the nooks get cleaned, and a smaller one the birds empty in about 2 to 3 days. UGA Extension says to select a feeder that is easy to clean and does not drip. FeederWatch notes that solution dripping onto a bee guard attracts wasps.

In basin feeders, FeederWatch says, the sugar water often sits too far in for a wasp to reach, while a hummingbird’s tongue still can. The [feeder guide](/feeders) compares shapes by how hard they are to scrub. The practical test is whether you will take that feeder apart on a hot afternoon.

## What if ants or bees have gotten into the feeder?

Empty it today. Audubon’s FAQ puts insects on the same line as mold and a sick bird: wash now, and discard the nectar. The [pest-control guide](/pest-control) covers ant moats and bees. Fish & Wildlife lists bees, ants, and other insects among feeder threats, beside spoiled nectar, and it points to an insect guard or an ant moat. Scrub the ports, dry the parts, and hang fresh nectar.
